What this is. A feed on lobbying worldwide, in twenty subjects. What is spent and by whom. The lobbyists themselves, and how many there are for each legislator. The shadow lobbying that roughly doubles those counts because it never registers at all. The revolving door, and the raise that comes with walking through it. Registers, disclosure regimes and what each country does and does not report — against the section's own figure that 18 of 195 countries regulate lobbying. Lobbyists writing the legislation. Access bought with donations, and whose preferences policy actually tracks. The bodies most lobbied, budget offices first among them. Trade associations. Front groups and astroturf. Funded research and captured expertise. Then the sectors the section singles out: climate obstruction, finance, food and drink, tobacco, pharma and gambling, technology. Plus lobbying by states and cities, lobbying on behalf of foreign powers, lawmakers' second jobs and undeclared interests, and what is set against all of it. Nothing here is written or summarised: headlines and snippets are the publishers' own, every row links to the original, and no model touches the text.

Two things the section separates that this wire keeps together. Its footnotes note that the presentation covered only corporations and industry associations, leaving out unions, non-profits and government bodies lobbying each other. This wire carries those too, in states, cities and governments lobbying, because a city hiring a firm to work the federal budget is the same machinery pointed a different way. And it treats funded research and captured expertise as lobbying rather than as a food-industry story, since paying for the study that decides the policy is influence bought earlier in the chain.

What it is not. A hotel lobby is not this subject, and neither is ordinary business or policy coverage with no influence angle — quarterly results, product launches, job cuts — nor the election horse race, which belongs to the voter wire. What this one keeps is who paid whom to shape the law. A story no subject will claim is refused rather than filed under a name it did not earn.

Standing. Every wire is labelled by what kind of source it is, and every row carries the label. Registrars, regulators & committees is the bodies that hold the registers and rule on breaches — lobbying commissioners and registrars, ethics and standards commissioners, the European Ombudsman, parliamentary committees, prosecutors, and the OECD on what each country discloses. Research & influence tracking is the organisations that measure it, including the climate policy assessments the section leans on. Transparency & accountability groups is those campaigning and litigating. Press is general news across 26 languages. Standing is provenance, not a claim that a story is important.

Weight. Separately, each story is scored on what it carries: a decision — a ruling, a register rule enacted, a fine, a suspension, a resignation, a ban (2); institutional material — a registrar or commissioner finding, an OECD or audit-office report, a disclosure filing, a peer-reviewed study (2); a measured figure such as a spending total, a meeting count or a ratio (1); a pending decision with a date attached (1); a named jurisdiction (1); and a primary source (1). At three or more the row is marked consequential.

What is refused. The other senses of the word, ordinary business and policy coverage with no influence angle, the election horse race, sponsored content and market copy. The status line reports how many stories each harvest refused.

Translation. The Translate control at the top renders the headlines and snippets on screen into any of 26 languages, through a free public translation endpoint — no account, no key, nothing stored. A translated row is marked as machine translation and keeps the publisher's original wording on hover, because a machine rendering is not the publisher's words. If the endpoint is unavailable the row simply stays in its original language rather than emptying. Translation applies to what is on screen; scroll further or widen a filter and press the control again.

Region. Placement runs three levels deep. Pick a region and a second row opens with its subregions; pick one of those and a third row lists the places inside it — Latin America to the Amazon Basin to the Brazilian Amazon, Oceania to Australia to the Northern Territory. Each row counts what it holds, so an empty subregion is visible rather than hidden, and every story files under the subregion and region above whatever place it names.

Limits worth knowing. The gate is mechanical: it reads words, not meaning. Standing is assigned per wire rather than per article. Google News caps a query at roughly 100 results over about 30 days. Coverage is uneven by language and the counts show it rather than hiding it.

Every wire read on the last harvest, with its standing: what it returned, and how many of its stories were refused. A wire that could not be reached says so rather than disappearing.
